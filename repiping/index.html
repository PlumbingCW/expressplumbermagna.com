Repiping replaces the supply lines inside your house when repairs no longer hold. The signs are repeated pinhole leaks, rust-colored water, low pressure at several fixtures and visible corrosion on pipes. It is a planned project, not an emergency, which means you can compare options calmly.

Signs Your Pipes Are at the End of Their Life

Pinhole leaks

Tiny spray or stains on copper, a sign of internal pitting.

Rusty water

Brown water after the tap sits, typical of galvanized steel.

Low pressure

Mineral and rust buildup narrows the pipe.

Frequent repairs

A second leak in a year points to the whole line.

Visible corrosion

Green or white crust on joints.

What Magna's Housing Stock Tends to Have

Houses built before the mid 1960s often have galvanized steel supply lines, which corrode inside. Homes from the following decades commonly used copper, and newer construction uses PEX or CPVC. Knowing the material tells you what to watch for.

The pipe in the wall is not always what the original plans say, since previous owners add repairs. A plumber checks accessible sections and the water heater connections to confirm.

Magna Water District's 2023 report showed moderately hard water with total dissolved solids near 639 mg/L. Minerals do not harm modern pipe, but they scale inside older steel and narrow it over time.

PEX is common in repipes because it snakes through walls with fewer cuts, and it tolerates the freeze-thaw swings better than rigid pipe. Copper remains a good choice where code or preference calls for it. The plumber explains what each means for your layout.

How a Repipe Goes

Assess

The plumber maps the system, checks pressure and finds access points.

Plan and price

You get a written scope and price before work begins.

Open and replace

Small access holes are cut and new lines run, fixture by fixture.

Test and patch

The system is pressure-tested, then drywall openings are patched or listed for a finisher.

Water is off for part of the day, so plan for that. Most homes finish in a few days depending on size. A repipe often follows a failed repair, as on the burst pipe repair page, or a detection visit through leak detection.

What Else Changes During a Repipe

A repipe is the right time to replace the shutoff valves, the pressure reducing valve and the hose bib connections, since the water is already off and the walls are open. Many plumbers also add a main shutoff that works smoothly and an accessible fixture stop at each sink and toilet. Those are cheap parts that save emergencies later.

Protecting Your Home During the Work

What to do before the crew arrives

  • Clear closets and walls near the access points.
  • Move furniture, art and fragile items away from work areas.
  • Tell the plumber about pets and alarm systems.
  • Plan for water to be off for part of each day.
  • Photograph walls and ceilings so you can compare after patching.

Drywall patches are usually left ready for paint, with texture matched by a drywall finisher. Ask in advance what the quote includes so you can plan a painter.

A Word About Water Pressure

High pressure shortens the life of any pipe. After a repipe, the plumber tests static pressure at a hose bib. If it runs above the range recommended by fixture makers, a pressure reducing valve brings it down. Replacing old pipe without fixing high pressure is how new pipe ends up leaking sooner than it should.

Code, Permit and Inspection

Repiping is permitted work in most places, and an inspector looks at the new lines before walls close. Utah applies the International Plumbing Code (IPC) with statewide amendments, and your city or county building department enforces it. The pipe material you choose must be approved for potable water in your jurisdiction, so ask your plumber to confirm that copper, PEX or CPVC is accepted where you live.

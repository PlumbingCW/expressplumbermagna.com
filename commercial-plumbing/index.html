After-Hours Commercial Emergencies

The most common after-hours commercial calls are grease-clogged kitchen drains, burst supply lines, overflowing toilets in public restrooms and water heater failures. Each can close a business until it is fixed, so we focus on stopping the damage first and then planning the full repair.

What Commercial Calls Look Like in Magna

Magna Main Street holds early twentieth century storefronts, and older commercial buildings often have mixed piping that has been patched over decades. Restaurants and shops along 3500 South and near SR-201 add food-service drains, grease traps and customer restrooms to the mix.

The older the building, the more likely a repair uncovers a hidden problem. A plumber who knows the building can plan access without disrupting tenants.

Grease Traps, Backflow Devices and Compliance

Restaurants usually need a grease trap or interceptor, and many cities require regular pumping. Ask your city or sewer district what applies to your kitchen. Backflow assemblies on fire lines and irrigation lines often need annual testing, and a failed test must be repaired.

We can arrange these as scheduled work, so they do not turn into emergencies. See drain cleaning for the routine side of kitchen drains.

Property Managers and Landlords

If you manage rental or commercial space, one call can handle after-hours tenant plumbing complaints. Tell dispatch you are calling for a tenant, give the unit address and the access contact, and the plumber works from there. For recurring buildings, ask about a regular service plan.

Tell dispatch these when you call about a business

  • The type of business and the fixtures affected.
  • Whether the building has a grease trap or interceptor.
  • Whether the floor drain or toilet is overflowing.
  • Who can give access and approve the quote.
  • Whether there is a landlord or building manager to notify.

Planning Ahead So You Do Not Close

Most commercial emergencies are preventable with a short walk-through. Locate the main shutoff and label it. Test the floor drains in the kitchen and restrooms by running water for a minute. Look at the water heater's relief valve discharge, the pipes in the ceiling and the backs of restroom cabinets for stains.

A monthly walk-through for a small business

  • Pour water into each floor drain and watch how fast it clears.
  • Check restroom fixtures for running toilets and slow sinks.
  • Look under sinks for drips, rust or damp cabinet floors.
  • Confirm the water heater is warm and not leaking.
  • Make sure staff know where the main shutoff is and how to close it.

When something does fail, the building's shutoff and an up-to-date contact list for the owner and landlord save time. A plumber who has seen the building once can plan faster the next time.

Tenants, Landlords and Who Pays

In leased space, the lease usually decides who handles repairs inside the suite and who handles the building systems. A clogged sink in a tenant space is often the tenant's job, while a failed building main is the landlord's. If you are not sure, call anyway to stop the damage, then sort out the bill with the lease in hand. Photographs and a written description of what happened help either way.

Codes and Inspections for Businesses

Commercial plumbing is held to the same model code as homes, plus extra rules for grease interceptors, backflow assemblies and accessible restrooms. Utah applies the International Plumbing Code (IPC) with statewide amendments, and your city or county building department enforces it. Your landlord, city and sewer district may add requirements for your kind of business, so confirm them before a remodel.

What to do before the plumber arrives

  • Close the building's main shutoff if water is spraying.
  • Move inventory and records off wet floors.
  • Keep customers and staff out of wet areas near electrical equipment.
  • Photograph damage for the landlord and insurer.
  • Tell dispatch who can open the building and approve work.
